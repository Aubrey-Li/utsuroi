Name

Utsuroi

移ろい

移ろい (うつろい, oo-tsoo-ro-ee) is the noun form of 移ろう, "to shift, to fade, to pass." In classical poetry it describes the changing of the seasons and the fading color of flowers. It's close to the idea of mono no aware, a gentle awareness that things pass.

It fits the app: words in sand, wind, and fire are utsuroi. The stones are what you choose to keep through it.

  • Nuance to know: 心の移ろい (a shifting heart) can suggest fickle feelings in love poetry. In this app's context it reads as change, not fickleness.
  • Similar-sounding word: 虚ろ (utsuro, without the final i) means hollow or vacant. Always keep the i in romanization.
  • Conflicts: no app or brand found using the name. There's an academic lab (Utsuroi Lab at Tokyo Denki University), a small three.js animation plugin on GitHub, and an unrelated horror app called "Utsuro's diary." Aubrey-Li/utsuroi is free on GitHub.

Using Japanese in the app

  • Home: 移ろい set vertically as the page's heading, with its meaning beside it. See Home.
  • Places: each is a single character, 波 (wave), 風 (wind), 火 (fire), 石 (stone), and 記 (record) for the journal, always with the English name under it.
  • Wordmark: lowercase "utsuroi" with 移ろい set small beside it.
  • Later: on first launch the stick writes 移ろい in the sand and the tide washes it away before the places appear.

The idea

Writing to let go, not to remember

A lot of writing is not about preserving a memory. It is a way to get out anger, regret, or the words you never said to a parent, a friend, or someone you lost. People already do this in nature: they write in the sand and let the waves take it, burn a letter, or carve a name into stone so it lasts. The app gives each of those acts a place. You choose the place, write, and the place handles your words.

  • Release is a gestureYou strike the match, blow the words away, turn toward the sea. No "Delete" button.
  • Released means goneReleased words are never saved to disk, sent to a server, or logged. Only the date and the ritual remain.
  • The medium shapes the wordsSand fits a sentence. A letter fits pages. Stone fits a few words, because carving is slow.
  • Painted, not photorealAn animated-game look in the spirit of Journey and The Witness: designed colors, soft bands of light, strong silhouettes.
  • It always feels handwrittenEven typed words arrive as strokes: traced in sand, carved by a chisel.
  • Never judgedThe app never reads, scores, or reacts to what you write. Support is always there, but quiet.

The four rituals

From a breath to forever

Each ritual sits at a different point on the permanence spectrum. Together the four cover the whole range, from words gone in a breath to words that stay.

Winda breath
Fireabout a minute
Oceana few waves
Stoneforever

Ocean

Released
Where you write
Wet sand at the waterline, at dusk.
How you write
By hand, anywhere on the beach. A driftwood stick follows your finger or mouse and draws grooves in the sand.
How much fits
The whole beach, but the closer you write to the water, the sooner it goes.
Release gesture
None. The sea is always coming in, the way it does on a real beach.
What happens
Most waves lap at the waterline. Now and then the swell builds and a bigger wave runs up and takes some of the words, nearest the water first. Washed lines can be written on again; when the sand is smooth everywhere, the words are forgotten.
Technique
Strokes, drawn or synthesized from typing, are stamped into a sand height buffer: the groove sinks and the sand piles into a low ridge on each side. A wave-coverage buffer builds up frame by frame (ping-pong render targets) and relaxes the height back to flat. Wet-sand darkening and a foam line in the shader.
Sound
Surf getting closer, distant gulls.

Wind

Released
Where you write
A meadow under a tree on a sunny day: blue sky, sunlight coming through the leaves.
How you write
Hand-drawing: traced in the air above the grass, leaving a trail of seed. Typing: letters gather out of drifting seed in a hand-lettered shape.
How much fits
A sentence or two.
Release gesture
Only when you confirm: Let the wind take it. (Blowing into the mic stays an idea for later.)
What happens
Letters loosen from their edges, lift, and scatter over the hills in the gust's direction. The last specks catch the light.
Technique
Sample points from the text mask into a GPU particle system (position and velocity textures), moved by curl noise plus the gust vector. The grass shader bends with the same gust. Breath detection uses a Web Audio analyser on low-frequency energy.
Sound
Wind that swells with your breath, rustling grass.

Fire

Released
Where you write
A blank sheet of paper on a desk in a dark room, lit by a single candle.
How you write
Hand-drawing: ink on the paper. Typing: a hand-lettered font, set as if written in ink.
How much fits
A full letter. The page scrolls.
Release gesture
When the letter is done, Light it: the candle's flame is carried to the corner of the page.
What happens
A burn front spreads from where you lit it. The paper browns, curls, and turns to ash, and glowing embers drift up into the dark.
Technique
Paper is a subdivided plane. Burn amount = distance from the ignition point + noise, compared against time: a brown char band, an emissive ember edge, transparency behind it. Vertices curl near the front. Ember particles and bloom.
Sound
Match strike, procedural crackle, then quiet.

Stone

Kept
Where you write
A quiet pebble shore in soft morning light. A flat stone lies in front of you; cairns of kept stones stand along the beach.
How you write
Typed. Each character is cut into the stone's top face as you type, with a chisel tap and a puff of dust.
How much fits
Up to four short lines. The letters get smaller as there's more, then the stone is full.
Release gesture
Set it on the cairn carries the stone to the top of a cairn, where it settles with a small wobble and a click.
What happens
The stone stays. A new cairn starts after eight. Drag to look along the shore; tap any kept stone to lift it toward you and read it, with the shore blurred behind it. Tap anywhere or Put it back to return it.
Technique
Stones are generated from a seed (a lumpy, flattened ellipsoid). The carving is a canvas groove texture that tilts the normal into each letter. The held view draws the shore through a Gaussian blur pass and the held stone sharp on top.
Sound
Small waves on pebbles, chisel taps, the stone set down.
Handwriting from typing

A stick that writes what you type

In the ocean scene, typed words shouldn't just appear in the sand. A driftwood stick writes them, letter by letter, the way a person would: it moves faster on straight lines, slows in tight curves, lifts between strokes, and digs a little deeper when it changes direction. The same stroke engine drives the chisel in the stone scene, so typed stones look carved by hand too.

How it works

Typed charactersQueued as you type, a few letters ahead of the stick
Single-line fontEach glyph stored as centerline strokes, not outlines
Natural variationSlant, size, baseline drift and per-point wobble, so no two letters match
↓
Timed stroke streamThe same format a finger produces on touch: points with position, pressure and time
↓
Stick or chiselVisible tool that follows the stream, tilts into its motion, casts a shadow
Sand or stoneGroove stamped into the height buffer as the tool passes

Details that make it feel human

  • Speed follows curvature. Human hand speed drops in tight curves (the "two-thirds power law" from motor-control research). Timing every point from local curvature makes it look written, not plotted.
  • Pen lifts. Between strokes the stick rises, travels through the air, and sets down again, leaving a small dent where it lands.
  • Pressure. Deeper at the start of a stroke and at sharp turns, lighter as it trails off.
  • Cursive joins. Lowercase letters connect where the font allows, so words flow instead of being stamped one glyph at a time.
  • Keeping up with typing. The stick writes at a natural three to five letters per second and speeds up a little if you type faster. A sentence finishes a moment after you stop.
  • Backspace. A letter not yet written just leaves the queue. A letter already written is smoothed away by a sweep of the stick's flat side.

Where the strokes come from

OptionHowTrade-off
Single-line fontsPlotter and engraving fonts already store centerline strokes (the Hershey fonts, and cursive single-line faces like EMS Allure). Convert once to stroke JSON.Simple and deterministic. Needs the variation layer to avoid looking mechanical. Check each font's license.
Skeletonized fontsRasterize any handwriting font and extract its centerline (thinning, then path tracing), with stroke order guessed.Works for any font, including non-Latin scripts. Stroke order can look wrong.
On-device synthesisA small handwriting-synthesis model (Graves-style recurrent network) generates pen strokes from text, running locally.Most natural and never repeats. Bigger download and a research spike. Not planned.
Your own handDraw the alphabet once with a finger or stylus, and the stick writes in your handwriting.Very personal. Needs a short setup step. Chosen as the alternative to the default cursive.

Decision: one carefully tuned cursive single-line font plus the variation layer is the default. You can replace it with your own handwriting (below). Japanese gets real stroke-order data from KanjiVG (kanji and kana, CC BY-SA 3.0, so the converted data files carry that license and need attribution). Chinese can use Make Me a Hanzi. For anything else neither covers, fall back to skeletonizing a regular font.

Your own hand

A one-time setup where you write the alphabet, after which the stick and the chisel write in your handwriting. It's available from settings and offered once after your first typed ocean entry.

  • Setup. Write each letter on a guideline: lowercase a–z first (enough to start), then optionally capitals, digits, and common punctuation. Around three minutes for lowercase. Uncaptured characters fall back to the default cursive.
  • Capture. Each letter is saved as timed strokes with pressure, normalized to a shared baseline and x-height. Writing a letter two or three times gives natural variety, and the engine picks one at random each time.
  • Joining. Your letters are written separately, so the engine adds short connecting strokes between lowercase letters when their end and start points are close, the way cursive joins form.
  • Timing. Your own recorded speed is used instead of the curvature-based timing, so the stick moves the way your hand moved.
  • Redo anytime. Rewrite any single letter you don't like.
  • Privacy. Your handwriting is personal and identifying. It's stored encrypted on the device like stones and included in backups.
The cairn

Stones that stack instead of disappearing

Stones can't be removed. Each one is placed on a cairn on the stone scene's shore, like the stacks people leave on beaches. The cairn is a slow record of what you chose to keep.

  • Placing. After carving, the stone is lifted onto the cairn and settles on top by itself, with a little wobble. Choosing a spot by hand could come later as an option.
  • Growing. When a cairn gets tall (around eight to ten stones), the next stone starts a new cairn beside it. Over years the shore fills with them.
  • Revisiting. Opening a stone day in the calendar takes you to the shore, with the camera on that stone so you can read it.
  • Stone shape. Stones are generated flat and smooth so they stack believably. Each is regenerated from a seed, so nothing heavier than a number is saved.
  • Physics. For the MVP, the resting spot is computed once (sample candidate positions on the top stone, pick the flattest contact) and animated as a settle. Real rigid-body physics (for example the Rapier engine compiled to WebAssembly) is a later option. The placement is saved, so the cairn never changes on reload.
Your days

The only trace is the ritual itself

A quiet list of every month you wrote in, newest at the bottom, like the end of a notebook. A day with anything in it is softly marked. Hover or tap it to see what happened. Released words were never kept, so those days only name the place that took them. Stone and journal days open.

  • Only months with something in them are shown, plus the current month.
  • No time of day or length is recorded for released rituals.
  • Leaving a scene before the release leaves no mark at all.
  • On wide screens the panel sits at the left of the home page; on phones it's a drawer behind the menu.

App flow

One ritual, start to finish

  1. Arrive. A short, quiet threshold: the last scene's ambience, one slow breath. Skippable.
  2. Choose a place. Ocean, wind, fire, or stone, before you write anything, or the plain journal. The scene sets the mood for the writing.
  3. Write. Inside the scene. Hand-drawing or typing, with a quiet switch in the corner. Nothing is autosaved.
  4. Release. The sea takes it on its own; the others when you choose: let the wind take it, light it, set it on the cairn.
  5. Stay a moment. The scene keeps playing after the words are gone. You leave when you're ready.
  6. Your days. Today is marked. Stone and journal days can be reopened.

Leaving early. A small "Leave" control is always visible while writing. Leaving discards the words, records nothing, and returns you to the scene picker. No "Are you sure?" prompt: nothing has been kept, so nothing is lost that you meant to keep.

Support. The menu always has a "Need to talk to someone?" entry with crisis lines for your region. It's never triggered by what you write, because the app never reads it.

Privacy model

What is stored, and what never is

Stored on the device

  • One mark per ritual: date and ritual type.
  • Stone entries: the strokes (drawn or synthesized), the source text if typed, the rock seed, and its place in the cairn, encrypted at rest.
  • Your handwriting alphabet, if you made one, encrypted at rest.

Never stored, anywhere

  • Released words or strokes.
  • Drafts, or anything from a scene you left early.
  • Any analytics event containing content or length.
  • Any analysis of content, including for support features.

Released text exists only in memory and GPU textures while the scene runs. Once the ritual ends, the canvas is cleared and the buffers are released. There is no server in the MVP.

// Stored for every ritual. No words, no time of day.
type RitualMark = {
  date: string;                 // local date, "2026-10-10"
  ritual: "ocean" | "wind" | "fire" | "stone";
  keptId?: string;              // set only for stone
};

// Stored only for stone. Encrypted with WebCrypto (AES-GCM).
type KeptEntry = {
  id: string;
  createdAt: string;
  strokes: Stroke[];            // what the chisel carved
  text?: string;                // source text, if typed
  stoneSeed: number;            // regenerates the same rock
  cairn: { index: number; level: number; pose: number[] }; // fixed resting spot
};

type Stroke = { points: [x: number, y: number, pressure: number, t: number][] };

// Optional. Your handwriting, several samples per character.
type HandAlphabet = { glyphs: Record<string, Stroke[][]> };

Backup file

  • Contents: calendar marks, stones, and your handwriting alphabet. Released words are never in it, because they were never stored.
  • Encryption: a passphrase you choose, stretched into a key (PBKDF2 with a high iteration count, or Argon2 via WebAssembly), then AES-GCM. Without the passphrase the file is unreadable, and we can't recover it.
  • Restore: open the file on any device and enter the passphrase. Stones merge into existing cairns in date order.
  • Reminders: one gentle suggestion after your first stone, and an optional reminder after every few new stones. No nagging.
  • Mobile: the Capacitor build saves through the system share sheet, so the file can go to iCloud Drive, Google Drive, or Files.
Technical approach

How it gets built

Platform

  • Web first: TypeScript + Vite, shipped as a PWA (installable, works offline, no account).
  • Mobile later: wrap the same build with Capacitor for native microphone access, haptics (match strike, chisel taps), and store distribution.
  • UI shell: a thin layer for menus and the calendar. Plain TS or a small framework, decided at repo setup.

Rendering

  • three.js WebGPURenderer with automatic WebGL2 fallback. Shaders written in TSL so one source runs on both.
  • The look: gradient skies, height fog, simple geometry with soft painterly textures, AgX tone mapping, bloom, light grain, and a vignette.

Text as material

The shared core. Strokes are the common format: hand-drawing produces them directly, and typing produces them through the stroke engine (ocean, stone) or a hand-lettered font (fire, wind). Everything ends up as the same mask texture and distance field, and each scene's shader or simulation reads from it. Build this once, then every ritual is a different consumer.

Hand-drawingPointer Events with pressure and coalesced points, smoothed into strokes. Works with mouse, finger, or stylus.
TypingHidden textarea for IME and accessibility, turned into strokes or hand-lettered glyphs
↓
Strokes → mask + SDFOffscreen canvas raster, distance field via jump flooding on the GPU
↓
OceanStrokes stamped into a height field, eroded by a wave-coverage buffer
WindPoints sampled into GPU particles, curl noise + gust
FireInk on paper, consumed by the noisy burn front

Audio

  • Web Audio graph per scene: layered ambient loops plus procedural crackle and gusts so nothing sounds looped.
  • Breath detection: an AnalyserNode on the mic, triggering on a sustained rise in low-frequency energy.
  • Audio starts on the first tap (browser autoplay rules), which can be the threshold moment.

Storage

  • IndexedDB for ritual marks and stone entries. Stone entries encrypted with a device-held key. A passphrase option comes later.
  • Call navigator.storage.persist() so the browser doesn't evict stones. Safari can clear storage for sites that aren't installed and haven't been visited for a week, so encourage installing the PWA.
  • Manual encrypted backup files in the MVP (see Privacy model). Automatic end-to-end encrypted sync is a later decision.

Performance on phones

  • Cap device pixel ratio at 2, or 1.5 on lower tiers. Simulation buffers at 512² or below.
  • Adaptive quality tiers chosen from measured frame time during the first seconds of a scene.
  • One scene loaded at a time, assets streamed per scene.
